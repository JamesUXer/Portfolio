Salient Bio: a clearer Learn Hub for microbiome testing

Patients needed clear explanations of testing and its personal relevance. Clinicians needed evidence and detail to interpret results and support patients. We designed a Learn Hub organised around user intent and testing stage.

My role
UX/Product Designer
Team / client
Design team for a UK biotechnology company

I led discovery research, mapped user flows and developed the information architecture and search approach. I contributed to prototyping and usability testing.

Apr 2026–May 2026 · End-to-end team project

I researched what patients and clinicians needed from the Learn Hub.

I interviewed two clinicians and one patient, alongside reviewing existing client research. Patients needed to judge whether testing was relevant to them and understand its implications. Clinicians needed evidence, methodology and resources to support discussions with patients.

Patients
Is testing relevant to me? What could it mean, and what happens next?
Clinicians

I organised the information around testing stages and professional needs.

I mapped patient journeys from considering a test to understanding results and next steps. Alongside those journeys, I developed a distinct clinician route, bringing professional information together within the same hub.

Explore the journeys and information architecture

How I organised the content

I grouped the architecture into General Overview, Testing Journey, Health Library and Clinician Resources. Within the testing content, I consolidated overlapping information around user needs and stages, while keeping search and direct access available across the hub.

The team translated that structure into guided navigation, searchable resources and patient and clinician content views.

Guided journeys and search served different starting points.

Someone exploring whether to take a test needed guidance. Someone looking for a specific answer needed direct access. I developed the search and retrieval approach alongside the navigation, so the concept could support both ways of finding information.

See the search and retrieval approach

Search alongside navigation

Search provided access across General Overview, Testing Journey, Health Library and Clinician Resources, without requiring someone to follow a complete testing journey.

I developed the retrieval approach and identified ambiguity in how the results view communicated what was active, suggested or most relevant. The team’s design response addressed three parts of the search experience.

Recognising the search state

Active filters
We separated applied filters from suggested terms, aiming to make the current search state easier to recognise.
Suggested terms
Possible refinements needed to be distinguishable from filters already in use, so people could tell what they might select next.

Giving the leading result more emphasis

Result priority
We gave the most relevant article stronger visual priority, rather than presenting all results with equal emphasis.

The next stage brings together the testing figures, my interpretation and the design comparisons.

Testing showed that finding an answer did not always mean the route was clear.

Participants took indirect paths and encountered difficulties with search controls and clinician resources. I simplified overlapping content in the architecture. We also made journey choices more visible, separated active filters from suggested terms and strengthened signposting to clinician resources. These revisions were not retested.

How I designed the usability study

I designed scenario-based tasks around risks identified during discovery and defined success criteria before testing. I assessed usefulness, efficiency and satisfaction using task completion, first-click success, path deviation, comprehension and confidence.

Make testing stages easier to recognise

What testing showed

71% of users found the information they needed to assess whether testing was suitable, but 71% also showed significant path deviation getting there.

My interpretation

The homepage gave search too much prominence while the guided routes were less visible. Finding an answer did not necessarily mean the journey was clear.

What we changed

I consolidated overlapping testing content around user needs and stages. We reduced search prominence and replaced expandable journey navigation with visible cards.

Still to validate: whether the revised homepage and information architecture help people find the right route. They were not retested.

Separate active filters from suggested terms

What testing showed

86% of users completed the search task successfully, but 71% encountered an issue with filters or chips.

My interpretation

I identified ambiguity in the retrieval model: people could reach relevant content, but the results view did not make clear what was active, suggested or most relevant.

What we changed

We separated active filters from suggested terms and gave the most relevant article stronger visual priority.

Still to validate: whether these changes make the search controls clearer. The revised search experience was not retested.

The proposed plain-English AI summary remained an unvalidated hypothesis. It needed clinical and content guardrails and further usability testing before any benefit to understanding could be established.

Clinician route and contextual support

Testing showed that professional users were not recognising where clinician-specific support lived. Only 33% achieved partial success, while 67% showed high path deviation; no participant found the clinician route without some uncertainty.

Discovery had identified clinicians’ need for evidence, methodology, suitability information and patient-support resources. I brought those requirements together in the architecture. We then grouped professional content under a more visible Clinician Resources route.

Supporting finding

Terminology without losing context

The same principle applied at a smaller scale to scientific terminology. Glossary definitions were useful once found, but only 33% fully completed the task and 67% showed significant path deviation.

We added clearer signposting to glossary terms and refined the in-context definition panel, aiming to make explanations easier to find without losing the reading context.

We delivered a Learn Hub concept with clear questions for further testing.

The next tests would check whether patients could find suitability information, refine searches and understand results and next steps. Clinician tasks would assess access to evidence, methodology and patient-support materials. Those findings would guide further development for both groups.

Next validation

Test access with both user groups

  • Patient starting points: can people considering testing recognise where to begin and find suitability information?
  • Search refinement: can people distinguish active filters from suggested terms and reach relevant content?
  • Results and next steps: can patients find explanations of results and identify what to do next?
  • Clinician resources: can clinicians find evidence, methodology and patient-support materials?

I would track task completion, path deviation, time on task and comprehension, and check where people believe education ends and medical advice begins. Those findings would guide further development for both groups.

Reflection

Different routes, a shared structure

The project strengthened my view that information architecture needed to reflect people's stage, intent and level of knowledge, while keeping direct routes available.

For the company, uncertainty about suitability, credibility or next steps could weaken confidence and cause people to leave the journey. This commercial risk remains a hypothesis, rather than a measured outcome.
