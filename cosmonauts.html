Cosmonauts: from commercial opportunity to user-centred experience

The team already understood a commercial opportunity. My contribution was to investigate how it could become a useful, understandable experience for sponsors.

My contribution: product review, sponsor discovery and early experience design.

Outputs: a research baseline, prioritised opportunities and a proposed journey prepared for testing. The revised experience had not yet been validated.

Discover

An opportunity already in motion

The team had a clear view of a commercial opportunity, product documentation and a working experience. I started by understanding that thinking and identifying which assumptions about people’s behaviour still needed evidence.

That distinction mattered. Existing product knowledge gave me a starting point; it did not answer every question about how sponsors would use the experience.

Existing knowledge
Commercial thinking and product documentation
User questions
Behaviour, routines and expectations to investigate
Experience decisions
Proposals to assess against that evidence

Define

Finding the questions worth investigating

I reviewed the existing experience and separated observations into issues that could be fixed, decisions that needed an owner and questions that required research. This gave immediate problems a route forwards while keeping genuine unknowns visible.

Discover

Focused discovery with real users

I scoped sponsor discovery to one live event and a focused set of questions about decisions and routines. The aim was to understand the context before testing interface choices.

The work offered directional evidence. It did not cover the full service, establish a representative picture or test the proposed onboarding. Those limits shaped what I could reasonably claim and what still needed investigation.

Define

Evidence informing experience direction

I brought together participant research, expert review and existing business thinking. These inputs answered different questions and carried different limits. A design response needed a reasoned argument, rather than simply being labelled “research-led”.

Understanding working practices

Participant researchNot yet validated
Starting question
How does the experience fit into sponsors’ working routines?
Evidence and its limits
Sponsor discovery at one live event. This provided directional evidence from a limited setting, without covering the full service or every user perspective.
My interpretation
The proposed experience needed to account for the context around a task, as well as what happened on screen.
Proposed action
Organise the proposed journey around meaningful preparation, evaluation and action. Use these stages to identify questions for testing.
Status and next test
A research-informed proposal, not a validated journey. The next test would examine whether the proposed tasks fit sponsors’ routines and support useful decisions.

Teaching through action

Expert reviewDesign proposalNot yet validated
Starting question
Could onboarding help people complete a meaningful task while learning the experience?
Evidence and its limits
One expert walkthrough and existing product thinking. Sponsors had not tested the proposed onboarding.
My interpretation
A task-led approach could make the experience easier to learn. This was my design judgement, rather than a finding established through participant research.
Proposed action
Explore guidance during meaningful tasks, then prepare a prototype and questions to test understanding.
Status and next test
A click-through prototype was prepared. It was not documented as shipped or tested with sponsors. The next test would examine whether people could understand and complete the proposed task.

Develop

Designing something testable

My walkthrough of the existing onboarding raised a question: was it helping people do a meaningful task, or mainly explaining navigation? I explored a more task-led approach that would guide people while they used the experience.

This was a design proposal grounded in expert judgement. Sponsor research had not established that the existing approach failed, and sponsors had not tested the alternative.

Explain before action

Introduce the interface, then ask people to apply what they have learned.

Guide during action

Offer relevant guidance while people work towards a meaningful task.

Hypothesis
Guidance during a task could make the experience easier to learn.
Test question
Can people understand and complete the proposed task?
Status
Prototype prepared. Not yet validated with sponsors.

Deliver

Keeping discovery connected to delivery

As the next iteration took shape, I began developing a repeatable way to gather structured feedback against specific design questions. The testing instrument was prepared, but the first participant round had not taken place at the time of this account.

The initial fieldwork logger and the later usability-test instrument served different purposes. Preparing an instrument was a step towards repeatable testing, rather than evidence that a continuous discovery programme was established.

What the work established, and what remained open

The work produced a structured research baseline, prioritised opportunities and a proposed journey prepared for testing. I still needed evidence of how sponsors would respond to the revised experience and which choices would hold up in use.

Established

  • A review that separated issues, decisions and research questions.
  • Directional sponsor discovery within a limited setting.
  • A proposed journey, prototype and testing instrument.

Still to learn

  • Whether the revised experience fits sponsors’ working practices.
  • Whether task-led guidance supports understanding.
  • How the design performs in use. No measured improvement was established.
