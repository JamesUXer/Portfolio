Why I designed this encounter

Five research interviews informed the early direction. The research highlighted the need for context and repeatable audio.

Characters introduce words through short conversations, with written text and speech. Children can listen again, hear the word on its own, or leave and return. The intention is to make help available without making every encounter a test.

These are design intentions. The prototype still needs testing with children.

Make room to change your mind.

Confidence and the response to mistakes mattered in the research. At the bench, children can move, swap and return letter blocks before trying their spell.

The interaction developed from selecting the next letter into rearranging the blocks directly. I kept judgement out of construction so children could inspect and revise their attempt.

Still to test: do children understand how to revise without an adult stepping in?

The spell becomes part of the story.

Tracing follows the constructed letters. The movement is forgiving, without an accuracy score, and children release between letters. Keyboard input offers another way through.

After a successful attempt, a question mark becomes an ink cat before returning to the pen. The reward connects to the meaning of curious.

Tracing and narrative are design choices here. Their contribution to spelling retention still needs testing.

The route changed as the idea developed.

Earlier work explored a parent-led spelling app. I removed teacher-assigned lists from that direction because depending on updated school lists was a parent pain point.

I also explored reducing parent setup with the Eliminate, Automate, Simplify framework. Those onboarding flows and the wider multi-stage learning plan are earlier explorations, outside this playable level.
